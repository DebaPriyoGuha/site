Speaker Script — Host-Galaxy Ages & the SN Ia Age Step

Presenter: Deba Priyo Guha (CASSA, IUB) · Supervisor: Dr. Syed Ashraf Uddin Deck: https://debapriyoguha.github.io/site/supernova-cosmology/ Style: easy, communicative English. Short sentences. Gloss every hard term once.

How to use this file - Each block is one slide, in deck order (31 slides: 1–20, 20b, 21–30). - SCREEN = what the audience is looking at (the proof). - SAY = your spoken words. Speak it, do not read it word for word. - POINT = point at this part of the plot / card. - Target: about 25–30 minutes. Roughly 45–60 seconds per slide.

The claim chronology in one breath (your spine for the whole talk): Gupta 2011 first sees a hint → Rose 2019 measures a real but modest age step → Chung 2025 and Son 2025 turn it into a huge bias that removes dark energy → Sah 2026 pushes it to the extreme → Wiseman 2026 rebuts it → we test it on new, independent data and the huge bias does not appear.

Slide 1 — Title

SCREEN Title, my name, supervisor, CASSA / IUB.

SAY "Good morning, everyone. I am Deba Priyo Guha, from CASSA at Independent University, Bangladesh. This is joint work with my supervisor, Dr. Syed Ashraf Uddin. Today's question is simple to state. Type Ia supernovae are the tool we used to discover dark energy. A recent paper claims that the age of the host galaxy fools that tool so badly that dark energy may not exist. I am going to test that claim on fresh, independent data, and show you, step by step, that the strong version of the claim does not hold."

Slide 2 — Motivation

SCREEN Four bullets, ending in the one-sentence card.

SAY "First, why this matters. Type Ia supernovae are standard candles. After we standardize their brightness, we treat them as fixed light bulbs and read off cosmic distances. The whole method assumes the brightness does not depend on which galaxy the supernova lives in.

In 2025, Son and collaborators challenged exactly that. They say there is a host-galaxy age bias so strong that, once you correct for it, the acceleration of the Universe goes away. That is a very big claim. And a big claim needs an independent check, because Son measured it on only one set of galaxies.

Our plan, in one line" POINT to card "is on the screen: use Rose's tool to test Son's claim, on data that Son never used."

Slide 3 — Summary (the whole story in one slide)

SCREEN Three roles: Rose the tool, Son the target, Wiseman the witness. Then eight numbered points.

SAY "Here is the whole story on one slide, so you always know where we are.

Three names to remember. Rose 2019 is our TOOL — his method to measure galaxy age. Son 2025 is our TARGET — the claim we push back on. Wiseman 2026 is our WITNESS — an independent group that says dark energy is safe.

The eight steps are the talk in order: Son says the age bias is strong enough to kill dark energy. But he never tested it on independent data. We reproduce Rose's method. We prove the method does not depend on the computer sampler. We run it on new CSP data with infrared. The strong bias does not come back. Wiseman independently agrees. And we deliver a public tool and three papers.

One line" POINT "measure with Rose, refute Son, backed by Wiseman."

Slide 4 — Son (2025): what he claims [TARGET]

SCREEN Red claim card with the numbers, then three bullets ending in "our plan".

SAY "Now the target in detail. This is Son, Lee, Chung and collaborators, 2025, in MNRAS. The paper is called 'Strong Progenitor Age-bias, Part Two.'

Their claim, in plain words: they find a very strong link — a five-and-a-half sigma link, meaning almost certainly real — between a standardized supernova's brightness and the age of the stars that made it. And they say the usual correction, the host-mass step, does not remove it, because age and mass change differently as we look back in time.

Then they take the big step. They correct the supernovae as a function of redshift, that is, as a function of look-back time. After that correction, the supernovae no longer need dark energy. Combined with other probes, they report more than nine sigma tension with the standard model, and a Universe that is currently not accelerating. Their phrase is, literally, 'no dark energy.'

POINT to the amber bullet "But here is the crack we build on. Son brought no new data. He re-measured ages on the same galaxies that Rose and an earlier 2011 sample already used. So the word 'everywhere' was never actually tested on an independent survey. That is exactly the gap we fill."

Slide 5 — Rose (2019): why we use it [TOOL]

SCREEN Teal claim card, then three bullets. This is the chronology's real starting point.

SAY "So let us go to the tool, and to where this whole idea really began: Rose, Garnavich and Berg, 2019, in ApJ. The nice title is 'Think Global, Act Local.'

Rose's honest result is on the card. After standardization, supernovae in older environments stay slightly too bright or too faint. He measures a real host-age step of about zero-point-one-one mag near eight billion years, at about three sigma. So age is a genuine third property, beyond the usual shape and colour.

Two things to notice. His signal is modest. The direct global correlation is only about two sigma. And although his title says 'local,' both he and Son actually use the age of the whole galaxy.

POINT to green bullet "So our stand is: we keep Rose's real global step, and we drop the 'act local' language that Son later inflates. The fight is not global versus local. Both sides use whole-galaxy age. The real fight is: how strong is the effect, and is it everywhere?"

Slide 6 — How mc-age works (the theory)

SCREEN Left: four bullets on Bayesian SED fitting. Right: the posterior formula box.

SAY "Quick theory, so the later plots make sense. mc-age is the name of Rose's code.

A galaxy sends out light in several colour bands. That set of brightnesses is its spectral energy distribution, its SED. We have a physics model, FSPS, that takes a few numbers — age, how fast stars formed, metals, dust, distance — and predicts the brightness in each band.

We compare the prediction to the data. A closer match means a higher likelihood. We add a prior, which just keeps the numbers physical. And a sampler explores all the combinations to give us the probability curve for the age.

POINT to box "In one line: old belief, times new evidence, gives updated belief. That updated belief is the posterior, and its peak is the galaxy's age. Rose's original sampler is called emcee."

Slide 7 — mc-age in action: SN762

SCREEN Left: observed magnitudes and two trial ages. Right: the best-fit SED plot (proof).

SAY "Here is one real example, so you see it work. This host, SN762, sits at redshift zero-point-one-nine. Look at the numbers: it is faint in the blue band and bright in the red band. That already whispers 'old, red galaxy.'

Now watch the fit. If we try an age of two billion years, the model is far too blue — rejected. If we try seven billion years, the model lines up with all five bands.

POINT to the plot "On the right is that best fit: the model curve sits right on top of the observed points. Our Nautilus run gives about seven-point-seven billion years. An old host, exactly as the colours promised."

Slide 8 — Trouble in mc-age → test six samplers

SCREEN Three bullets, then six sampler cards.

SAY "But there is a practical problem. Rose's sampler, emcee, is slow. For one galaxy it runs the physics model over two million times, so each supernova takes hours.

That raises a scientific worry, not just a speed worry. If I change the search tool, does the age change? If it does, then the age is an artefact of the tool, and I cannot trust it.

So we did the honest thing. We ran the same physics model with six different search tools" POINT to cards "and checked they all land on the same age. Five are samplers; the sixth, BAGPIPES, is a completely different code we use as a cross-check later."

Slide 9 — Nautilus: the theory

SCREEN Left: three bullets. Right: two cards ("the clever part", "bonus: evidence Z").

SAY "One sampler won this race, so let me explain it simply: Nautilus.

It uses nested sampling. You scatter many test points across the whole range. Each round you throw away the worst point and shrink the search inward. Like a nautilus shell spiralling to its centre, it closes in on the best answer.

Two advantages. It naturally finds two-peak cases, where emcee's random walk often gets stuck on one peak. And" POINT to bonus card "it gives the 'evidence' for free — a single number that lets us compare whole models. One caution: the helper model inside Nautilus is just a faster way to search. It is not deep learning on the data."

Slide 10 — Why Nautilus: the race [PAPER 1 result]

SCREEN Left: interactive timing bar chart (proof). Right: four bullets.

SAY "Now the proof that Nautilus wins. Same setup, six tools, one machine. The question: who finishes the most galaxies in twelve hours?

POINT to the bars "On a single object, SN762, Nautilus is fastest in both real time and computing cost — about zero-point-three hours, roughly two times cheaper than the next tool, and about sixty times cheaper than emcee.

On throughput, with six cores in twelve hours, Nautilus finishes eleven hosts, the next tool nine, and emcee and dynesty finish zero. It wins because it prunes bad regions early and reuses its helper model, so it needs far fewer physics calls. This race is the core of our first paper."

Slide 11 — Cross-checks & the age–mass degeneracy

SCREEN Two interactive scatter plots (proof): left vs Rose, right vs Gupta. One-to-one lines.

SAY "Speed is nothing if the answer is wrong. So do our ages agree with published ages? Yes.

POINT to left plot "On the left, our Nautilus ages against Rose's, for ninety-seven shared hosts. The points track the one-to-one line within their error bars. The small offset is under one billion years.

POINT to right plot "On the right, against an even earlier catalogue, Gupta 2011. Again consistent.

One honest caution — the degeneracy. From broad colours alone, an old metal-rich galaxy and a younger dusty galaxy can look the same. Photometry cannot fully separate them. Infrared bands help a lot, and spectroscopy is the true fix. This is exactly why CSP, with its infrared, matters next."

Slide 12 — Gupta (2011): the cross-check paper [FOR US]

SCREEN Green claim card, two bullets. This is the earliest link in the chronology.

SAY "Let me name that cross-check paper properly, because it is the start of the timeline: Gupta and collaborators, 2011.

This was one of the first measurements of host ages by SED fitting for these supernovae, using a different pipeline and different stellar models from Rose. And even back then, there was an early hint that older hosts leave a small residual after standardization.

POINT to green bullet "For us it is a second, independent anchor. On the thirty-three hosts we share, our ages and Gupta's agree to within about half a billion years. So our ages are not a quirk of one code."

Slide 13 — The Carnegie Supernova Project (CSP)

SCREEN Left: three bullets (what / why / SDSS→CSP). Right: teal logic card.

SAY "Now the heart of the talk: the independent test. The Carnegie Supernova Project, or CSP.

What it is: CSP-One has one hundred twenty-six Type Ia supernovae from a one-metre telescope in Chile, at low redshift. Crucially, it has optical bands and near-infrared bands, Y, J and H.

Why it is the right test: these host galaxies are largely outside the samples Son used. And the infrared breaks the degeneracy I just showed you.

POINT to card "So the logic is clean and fair. If Son's bias is truly strong and everywhere, it must show up in CSP. If it disappears on independent infrared data, then the claim does not generalize."

Slide 14 — Filter curves: why one band reads two values

SCREEN Left: three bullets on transmission curves. Right: card + note.

SAY "A short but important technical point, because it decides whether the fits work at all.

A filter curve is simply how much light a band lets through at each wavelength. A magnitude is the galaxy's light folded through that curve. The catch: the 'u' band on SDSS and the 'u' band on CSP are different curves, so they read slightly different numbers. And CSP even swapped physical filters mid-survey, so the right curve depends on the date the supernova was observed.

POINT to card "So we register the correct CSP filters into the model and apply the right offsets. Get the filter wrong, and the age fit rails — it slams into an edge and gives nonsense. That is the next slide."

Slide 15 — mc-age on CSP: SN04dt, our first host

SCREEN Left: the correction equation and the AB offsets. Right: results table (proof).

SAY "Here is our first CSP host, and it shows why those corrections matter.

CSP magnitudes are in a natural system, not the standard AB system. So before fitting, each band gets one correction: remove Milky-Way dust, then add an AB offset. Notice the infrared offsets are large — J is plus zero-point-nine, H is plus one-point-three.

POINT to table "Watch the table. With no correction, the fit rails: an impossible age of twelve, a broken metallicity. Add the AB offset — it becomes sensible. Add Milky-Way dust removal too — and we land on a clean final age of about ten-point-four billion years, well converged. An old elliptical host, exactly what its red colours imply. The infrared offsets are what turned a broken fit into a clean one."

Slide 16 — Uddin et al. (2024): our data foundation [FOR US]

SCREEN Green claim card, two bullets.

SAY "Our Hubble residuals do not come from us. They come from this paper: Uddin, Burns, Phillips and collaborators, 2024, in ApJ. This is the CSP measurement of the expansion rate.

What it delivers: peer-reviewed, standardized distances, Hubble residuals, and host masses for the CSP sample. It is the solid ground our test stands on.

POINT to green bullet "Our contribution is to split the published CSP residuals by our independent infrared ages, and look for the age step. And the published CSP masses anchor our mass cross-checks, which come next."

Slide 17 — Host age vs redshift (interactive)

SCREEN Interactive scatter (proof): age versus redshift, CSP-I and CSP-II.

SAY "Now four slides of our actual CSP results. First, the ages against redshift. Each dot is one host galaxy with its error bar. You can drag to zoom. The point to take away: we have real, measured ages across the whole CSP redshift range, from two independent sub-samples, CSP-One and CSP-Two."

Slide 18 — Host-galaxy age distribution (interactive)

SCREEN Interactive histogram (proof) of host ages.

SAY "Same ages, shown as a distribution. And here is a fact to hold onto for the result at the end: CSP hosts skew old. The median is about nine billion years, and there are very few young systems. Remember this — it matters for how big any age step can even be."

Slide 19 — Nautilus: mass & fit vs published CSP (interactive)

SCREEN Two interactive plots (proof): left mass vs published CSP mass, right observed vs model magnitudes, one-to-one lines.

SAY "Are the fits themselves good? Two checks.

POINT to right plot "On the right, observed versus model magnitude for every band. The points sit on the one-to-one line, so the model reproduces the real photometry.

POINT to left plot "On the left, our stellar mass against the published CSP masses. They track, but our mass sits a bit high. That is expected: our code reports the mass of all stars ever formed, while the reference reports the mass of stars still living. So the message is: trust mc-age for age. For mass, we cross-check with a second code."

Slide 20 — Why BAGPIPES: mass & fit vs published CSP (interactive)

SCREEN Two interactive plots (proof): BAGPIPES mass and observed-vs-model.

SAY "That second code is BAGPIPES. Why bring in another code suddenly? Because mc-age is excellent for age, but its mass is biased high, as I just said.

BAGPIPES is completely different — different stellar models, different star-formation history — and it reports the living stellar mass. The logic is simple: if a totally different code still agrees, then the result is real physics, not a bug in one program. One caution: a few masses are placeholder-capped, and we have flagged those for review."

Slide 20b — Host mass vs star-formation rate

SCREEN Interactive plot (proof): mass vs SFR for all 332 BAGPIPES hosts, offset method, main-sequence fit line, floor line.

SAY "One more picture of the hosts: mass against star-formation rate, for all the host galaxies. Here it is — all three hundred thirty-two BAGPIPES hosts.

POINT to the sloping points and dashed line "The star-forming galaxies follow the 'main sequence.' It just means more massive galaxies form more stars. Our fitted slope is about one-half, which is the expected trend.

POINT to the floor line "But look at the bottom line. Many CSP hosts are 'quiescent' — they have basically stopped forming stars, so their rate is almost zero. The logarithm of zero is undefined, so I would normally lose them. To keep every galaxy on the plot, I add a tiny number before taking the log — the offset method — so all the dead hosts line up neatly at the floor instead of vanishing.

The take-away agrees with slide eighteen: about half the hosts sit on that floor. CSP hosts are old and passive. Keep that in mind for the final result."

Slide 21 — The Hubble-residual age step [the key test]

SCREEN Left: the Tripp standardization formula and the result. Right: interactive HR-vs-age plot with young/old means (proof).

SAY "So, does an age step appear in CSP? This is the key test.

We take the published CSP Hubble residuals — standardized with the Tripp formula on the left — and split the hosts by our own independent Nautilus global ages, young versus old, at eight billion years.

POINT to the two horizontal lines "The young mean and the old mean sit almost on top of each other. The step is minus zero-point-zero-two, plus or minus zero-point-zero-three magnitudes — that is well under one sigma, on a hundred and eighty-one CSP supernovae.

POINT to the result "In plain words: on independent CSP data, the global host age does not bias the supernova distances. So Son's strong, cosmology-killing step does not reproduce. This is exactly what our thesis predicts."

Slide 22 — Who is for us, who is against [the debate]

SCREEN Three-row table: for us / half-ally / against us.

SAY "Let me place everyone in the debate on one table.

For us: Rose, Gupta and Uddin — age is real but modest, an independent age check, and the CSP residuals. Against us: Son, and the papers that extend him — Sah, Lee, Chung — the strong, everywhere bias that removes dark energy.

POINT to middle row "And an interesting middle case: Wiseman 2026. He is with us on the big thing, dark energy is safe. But he says age is negligible, while we and Rose say age is real but small. So our exact line is:" POINT "age is real, and dark energy is safe — which rejects Son's over-reach."

Slide 23 — Wiseman (2026): the witness [FOR US]

SCREEN Green claim card with three sub-points, then two bullets.

SAY "Now the witness in detail: Wiseman and collaborators, 2026, in MNRAS. This is an explicit rebuttal of Son, and this is where the chronology swings back.

Three points on the card. One: Son leaves out the host-mass correction; put it back, and the age dependence vanishes. Two: the survey they analyse measures the evolution of the host effect as consistent with zero — it moves the dark-energy parameter by less than one percent. Three: the big age gap Son relies on is overstated several times over, because he confuses the galaxy's age with the progenitor's age.

POINT to bullets "So Wiseman is our ally against Son's cosmology. He is only a half-ally on the smaller point: he calls age negligible once mass is controlled, while we and Rose find a small but real global age step. Our line stays: age real at fixed redshift, and dark energy safe."

Slide 24 — Sah (2026): Son pushed further [AGAINST US]

SCREEN Red claim card, two bullets. This is the extreme end of the chronology.

SAY "For completeness, the most extreme point against us: Sah, Rameez and Sarkar, 2026, in MNRAS.

They take Son's age correction and apply it to the large Pantheon-plus sample, band by band in redshift. After that, the deceleration parameter flips sign — the Universe comes out decelerating. This is the strongest pro-Son cosmology on the table.

POINT to red bullet "Our answer is the same as before, only sharper. A bias strong enough to flip the whole expansion must appear in independent, infrared-anchored CSP ages. It does not. And even Sah notes that Wiseman's mass correction removes the age–residual link. Their ages carry the very degeneracy our infrared breaks."
